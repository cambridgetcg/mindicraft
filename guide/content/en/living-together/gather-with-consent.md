Every household knows things no other household knows: which field floods in a wet spring, which seed did best in the dry year, how high the river came in the great flood, how many baskets each field gave. Put together across a valley, that knowledge shows what no single house can see — whether there is grain enough for a hard winter, where the river is rising, which seed to plant next year.

But what a household knows is theirs, as much as their grain is. Copy it without asking — a tally read over a fence, a count repeated at market, a family's trouble passed along — and people stop telling anything, to you or to anyone who comes after you. So the craft here is not getting the knowledge. It is asking for it, keeping to exactly what was agreed, and leaving people glad they said yes.

The one rule is short: **ask first**. If you are not sure you may take something, ask the people who keep it, and wait for their answer.

## You need

- **One clear sentence of what you want and why** — "how many baskets your barley field gave this autumn, so the valley council can plan a winter store." If you can't say it plainly, you are not ready to ask.
- **Your record book** — the one book you keep for stores and debts, with a separate page for each household or village you gather from, so their facts can be handed back if they ask.
- **A way to make two copies of an agreement** — two written sheets, one for them and one for your book.
- **Your own council's word, if you gather for the village** — so that when you say "our council asks", it is true.
- **Time to wait** — a household may want to sleep on it; a village answers only when its council meets.

## Steps

1. **Know exactly what you want, and why, before you ask.** Write it down: what knowledge, from whom, what you will use it for, and who else will ever hear it. Ask for no more than that purpose needs. Every extra question is a burden on them and one more thing for you to keep safe.
2. **Ask openly, and say who you are and why.** Go yourself, by daylight, under your own name. Tell them who you are; who you are asking for — yourself, or your council; what you want; what you will do with it; who else will hear it; when and how often you would come; and that they can tell you to stop at any time. Ask the person who speaks for the household. For something the whole village keeps — its rain record, its count of the common flock — ask its council, and wait for it to meet and decide.
3. **Wait for a clear yes.** Only a yes is leave. Silence is not a yes. Neither is a shrug, a "maybe", or a child of the house saying "I'm sure they won't mind." If no answer comes, you have no leave: leave that thing out. If they say no, thank them and go. A no is an answer, not a problem to get around.
4. **Write down exactly what they agreed to, and keep it.** Before you take anything, write: which household or village agreed, and who spoke for it; the day; exactly what you may take; and each use, one by one — for your own planning, to tell at market, to put into a sum with other households' counts, to pass to another village. Each use is separate: leave to plan with a count is not leave to tell it. Write how long the leave lasts ("until next harvest", "three years"), any conditions (which days to come, how they want to be named), and that they may say stop at any time. Read it aloud, both of you make your marks, and keep one copy each. Give the agreement a short name — "Ford village, first agreement" — and if it ever changes, give the new one a new name.
5. **Take only what they agreed, gently.** Come on the days they chose, never in their busiest week. Take only the things named: leave to hear their barley count is not leave to count their store yourself, or to ask about the rye. Keep each visit short. Send one asker, not several: three people asking the same house in the same week is three times the burden, even if nobody meant it. If they say "not today", stop and go at once. If "not today" comes two or three times running, take it as a no, and ask them plainly whether you should come again. If you are unsure whether something is covered, ask them — not your friends, not your own council — and leave it undone until they answer.
6. **Write down where each fact came from, and when.** Each fact gets its own line on their page: what it is, which household or village told it, the day it was true ("this autumn's harvest"), the day you were told, and the name of the agreement it came under. Write it as they said it, in their own measure — "eleven Ford baskets" — not turned into yours. If they could not or would not tell you something, write "not told". Never write zero: zero says they had none; "not told" says you don't know.
7. **Never take what people keep private about themselves.** Who is sick, who owes whom, who quarrels with whom, what was said inside someone's house — none of it goes into your book, even when someone offers it, even when you overhear it, even when it would make your count better. No one can give you leave for another person's private matters: not their household, not the council, not their friend. If you hear such a thing while you gather, let it go then and there: never write it, never repeat it. If what you need is mostly about people themselves, this guide is not your road. Use only what the village already tells everyone with no names in it — "six are sick this week, across the village" — or leave it out.
8. **When you add others' counts together, don't let your sums give away what they asked you to keep.** A household may let its count go into a sum — the valley's whole barley harvest — but ask that its own count be told to no one. Then:
   - Use only counts whose keepers agreed they may go into a sum. Putting a count into a sum never makes it right to use a count you had no leave for.
   - Wait until enough households have given. Set the smallest number in council beforehand — ten households is a fair start — and say it out loud. Households whose counts are already told openly at market don't add to that number. A sum of only two gives each one away: anyone who knows one share can work out the other. Often this means telling nothing yet. Say so, rather than tell a sum that gives a household away.
   - Never tell the biggest, the smallest, or the middle share: each one is a single household's own count.
   - Never tell a second sum that leaves one household out: the difference between the two is that household's count.
   - Round what you tell — "about four hundred baskets" — and name who gave, never how much each gave.
   - Say how many gave and that the rest did not, so a sum of twelve households is never taken for the whole valley.
9. **Credit them, correct your mistakes, and give back.** Every time you use or tell a fact, say where it came from and what it is: "Ford's own count of their barley, told after harvest." When you find you copied or added something wrong, cross it out with one line, write the right one beside it, and tell them and everyone you told. And give back: tell them what you did with what they gave, show them any mistake you found in their own count, and, once it may be told (step 8), share the sum their count helped make.
10. **When they say stop, stop.** That same day, stop taking, stop telling their facts, and stop telling any sum their counts went into. Don't tell a new sum without them in its place: anyone who remembers the old one could subtract and find their count. Wait for fresh counts next season. If they ask for their facts back, hand back or burn their pages, and write one line in your record book — "Ford's pages returned at their asking" — with the day. This is the one time you take something out instead of crossing it out, and you do it openly. Then tell them it is done.

## Watch out

- **Taking silence for a yes.** "They never said no" is not leave. Only a clear yes is.
- **Going around a no.** Asking someone else in the house behind the back of the one who said no, sending a friend to ask without saying it is for you, reading their tally while they are out, coming back under another name, or copying it from someone who took it without asking. Each one overrides their answer and closes the door for every asker after you. A shut gate or a "not now" is an answer too. A no stands until they themselves change it.
- **Your own side's order is not their leave.** If your council, an elder or a friend tells you to take it anyway, the answer is still no. Only the people who keep a thing can give leave for it, and no one can give leave for another person's private matters. Say so plainly, and take nothing.
- **Leave for one use stretched to cover another.** Leave to plan with it is not leave to tell it at market; leave for this year is not leave forever. Check the written agreement, not your memory of it.
- **Using what you had before they agreed.** Their yes covers what you take from that day on. Anything you heard or copied before stays out of your book and out of your sums.
- **Adding baskets that are not the same basket.** Ford's basket and the hill village's basket may hold different amounts. Add counts only in the same measure, or turn them into one measure openly and write down how you did it.
- **A sum that tells more than it should.** A sum of two, the biggest share, or a sum told again with one household missing each gives one household's count away. When in doubt, don't tell it.

## How it works

Knowledge that is given comes again. Knowledge that is taken stops coming. A household that was asked first, told exactly what would happen to its count, saw you keep to it, and knew it could stop you at any time has every reason to say yes next time — and to tell its neighbours that saying yes to you is safe. A household whose count was spread at market, worked out from a careless sum, or whose private trouble was repeated stops telling anyone, and the next asker pays for your carelessness. The written agreement, the line for every fact, and the careful sums do one job: they let anyone see, fact by fact, that you stayed inside what you were given.

## Go further

- **Try it small this week.** Ask one neighbour for one thing they know — how deep their well is, or which seed did best in last year's rain. Say exactly why, write down what they agreed to, and go back to show them what you did with it. A no teaches you how to ask, too.
